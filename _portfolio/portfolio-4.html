Overview

The Stokes–Einstein–Debye (SED) relations connect how fast molecules diffuse and rotate to the structural relaxation of the liquid around them. In supercooled glass-forming liquids they break down, and two distinct mechanisms are responsible: translational–rotational decoupling, and the failure of the Debye model of rotational diffusion. Disentangling them is needed for a microscopic picture of rotational dynamics near the glass transition.

Schematic of the geometry (not simulation data): a dense supercooled liquid slab coexisting with a dilute vapour, formed by cavitation at ρ = 0.8.

Model & method

  • Molecular dynamics of a binary mixture of dumbbell molecules, bonded by the finitely extensible nonlinear elastic (FENE) potential and interacting through a standard glass-forming Lennard-Jones potential.
  • At a reduced number density ρ = 0.8 the system cavitates into a dense liquid slab coexisting with a dilute vapour phase — a phase-separated, heterogeneous environment.
  • The contributions of Debye-model breakdown and translational–rotational decoupling to the SED violation are separated and compared with the homogeneous liquid at ρ = 1.2.

Key finding

The two mechanisms have opposite density dependences: the Debye model breaks down more strongly at the higher density (ρ = 1.2), while translational–rotational decoupling is more pronounced at the lower density (ρ = 0.8), where cavitation introduces structural and dynamical inhomogeneity.
